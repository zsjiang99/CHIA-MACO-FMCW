\begin{abstract}
Frequency-modulated continuous-wave (FMCW) radar processing comprises windowing, fast Fourier transforms, power accumulation, and neighborhood-based detection, which exhibit distinct computational and data-movement characteristics. Coarse-grained reconfigurable arrays (CGRAs) can accommodate this heterogeneity by configuring their computational resources for individual pipeline stages. Identifying an effective end-to-end design, however, requires joint exploration of architectural resources and compiler transformations within a large, interdependent design space. We present RACO, an agentic framework for FMCW acceleration on CGRAs that organizes design-space exploration into four stages: HW/SW Co-design, Error Correction, Best-Design Selection, and Evaluation \& Feedback. Within this loop, CHIA executes the Evaluation \& Feedback stage: it compiles and maps five FMCW kernel views with an LLVM-based CGRA toolchain, converts the mapper-reported initiation intervals into an estimated cycle count for the complete frame, and records design validity and aggregate cost for later co-design rounds. In a recorded FMCW case study with a common budget of 10 unique kernel-mapper evaluations, RACO achieved an estimated steady-state cost of 38.73 million CGRA cycles per frame, compared with 42.14 million for the HW-only ablation and 41.45 million for the single-agent one-shot baseline.


\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Frequency-modulated continuous-wave (FMCW) radar estimates target range and velocity by processing sequences of sampled chirps. A typical range--Doppler pipeline performs windowing, range and Doppler FFTs, data transposition, power accumulation, and cell-averaging constant-false-alarm-rate (CA-CFAR) detection. These stages exhibit distinct computational and data-movement patterns, ranging from elementwise arithmetic and FFT butterflies to data reordering, channel reduction, and neighborhood scans. Coarse-grained reconfigurable arrays (CGRAs) can accommodate this diversity by configuring their processing elements and interconnects for the computational and dataflow requirements of each stage.

% Despite this flexibility, identifying an effective CGRA configuration for the complete FMCW pipeline remains challenging. The architecture space spans array dimensions, functional-unit allocation, memory organization, and configuration capacity, while the compiler space includes per-kernel transformations such as loop unrolling and vectorization. These decisions are tightly coupled: compiler transformations alter the parallelism and dataflow graph presented to the mapper, whereas architectural resources constrain how efficiently the resulting graph can be placed and scheduled. Moreover, because one architecture must support the entire pipeline, a resource allocation that benefits one kernel may provide limited benefit to another. Effective end-to-end design therefore requires joint HW/SW exploration guided by whole-pipeline evaluation.
Despite this flexibility, finding an effective CGRA configuration for the complete FMCW pipeline remains challenging~\cite{tan2022asap,tan2021opencgra}. As is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:challenges}, the challenge arises from three factors. \textbf{First}, selecting a shared architecture and compatible per-kernel compiler settings requires expertise in both hardware and compiler design. \textbf{Second}, the joint design space spans array dimensions, functional-unit and memory allocation, configuration capacity, loop unrolling, and vectorization. These choices are tightly coupled: compiler transformations reshape the parallelism and dataflow graph presented to the mapper, while architectural resources determine whether the graph can be mapped and at what initiation interval. Because all five kernels share one architecture, resources that benefit one stage may provide little improvement in whole-frame cost. \textbf{Third}, each candidate must be compiled and mapped across the five kernel views, while failed mappings consume the evaluation budget without yielding a viable design. Manual tuning and exhaustive evaluation therefore scale poorly, motivating a search process that uses whole-pipeline feedback to guide subsequent exploration.

To address this challenge, we present RACO, an open-source multi-agent framework for CGRA HW/SW co-design of FMCW radar pipelines. Rather than treating design-space exploration as a monolithic process, the framework decomposes the workflow into four stages: \textit{HW/SW Co-design}, \textit{Design Error Correction}, \textit{Best Design Selection}, and \textit{Evaluation \& Feedback}. These stages respectively generate complete HW/SW designs, correct invalid configurations, identify high-quality candidates, and evaluate their mapping outcomes. Each design combines one CGRA architecture shared across the pipeline with compiler transformations specified for the five FMCW mapping views. CHIA implements the Evaluation \& Feedback stage by compiling and mapping these views with an LLVM-based CGRA toolchain, estimating complete-frame cost from mapper-reported initiation intervals, and recording design validity and aggregate cost for the next design round. The resulting closed loop grounds subsequent design decisions in observed mapping behavior.


We evaluate RACO against a hardware-only ablation and a single-agent one-shot baseline under the same budget of 10 unique mapper evaluations. RACO attains an estimated steady-state frame cost of 38.73 million CGRA cycles, 8.1\% lower than hardware-only (42.14 million) and 6.6\% lower than single-agent one-shot (41.45 million). We contribute an executable FMCW co-design task spanning five kernel views, a closed feedback loop that links agent decisions to CHIA~\cite{cui2026chia} mapper evaluations through a frame-level mapping objective, and an open-source artifact containing the complete workflow, decision traces, and raw mapper logs.


\section{Background}
In this section, we discuss the background of FMCW Radar processing and CGRA HW/SW design.

\subsection{FMCW Radar Processing}
An FMCW radar transmits frequency-swept chirps; after mixing the received echoes with the transmitted waveform, beat frequency encodes target range and phase progression across chirps encodes radial velocity. A range--Doppler pipeline applies windowing and a range FFT within each chirp, transposes the intermediate data, and performs a Doppler FFT across chirps~\cite{li2021signal}. Power accumulation combines the receiver channels, and CA-CFAR detects targets by comparing each range--Doppler cell with its local background~\cite{rohling1983radar}. These stages span elementwise arithmetic, butterfly computation, data reordering, channel reduction, and neighborhood aggregation. We represent the pipeline with five mapping views—windowing, FFT, transposition, power accumulation, and CA-CFAR—with the FFT view shared by the range and Doppler transforms.

\subsection{CGRA HW/SW Co-Design}
As shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:wide_background}, CGRA HW/SW design spans compiler (software) and architecture (hardware) spaces. A CGRA fabric consists of a two-dimensional grid of tiles integrating functional units, local memory, and programmable interconnects. Architectural choices determine the array dimensions and resource allocation, while compiler choices, such as loop-unrolling and vectorization factors, affect how efficiently each kernel maps onto the fabric. Effective CGRA design therefore requires joint HW/SW optimization.

The strong interdependence among architectural parameters, compiler choices, and performance objectives makes identifying an effective CGRA design both time- and effort-intensive~\cite{weng2019daegen,weng2020dsagen}. This challenge is particularly pronounced for FMCW processing, where five mapping views with distinct computational and data-movement patterns must share a single CGRA architecture. RACO addresses this challenge through an agentic HW/SW co-design loop guided by compiler and mapper feedback.

\section{RACO Framework}
This section introduces the RACO, a LLM-based multi-agent system tailored for CGRA HW/SW co-design.
\subsection{Workflow Overview}

% As shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:workflow},

RACO organizes CGRA HW/SW exploration as a four-stage closed loop comprising \textit{CGRA Design}, \textit{Validation \& Correction}, \textit{Multi-Judge Selection}, and \textit{Performance Evaluation \& Feedback}. The first three stages generate, repair, and select complete HW/SW candidates, while the fourth stage uses CHIA to evaluate their mapping behavior and return tool-derived feedback. Repeating this process enables subsequent design decisions to respond to observed mapper outcomes.

\subsection{CGRA Design}

A domain-aware \textbf{CGRA Co-designer} generates candidate designs spanning both architectural and compiler parameters for FMCW acceleration. The agent is provided with CGRA HW/SW co-design principles, FMCW workload characteristics, the legal design space, and the optimization objective, enabling it to reason about the interactions between architectural resources and compiler transformations. Each proposal specifies a complete HW/SW configuration for subsequent validation and evaluation.

Across design iterations, the Co-designer receives prior candidate configurations, validity status, and aggregate frame costs from CHIA evaluation. This feedback lets the agent reconsider failed designs or poor pipeline-level estimates. Candidate generation can therefore respond to observed tool outcomes rather than relying solely on the LLM's initial predictions.

\subsection{Validation \& Correction}

Candidate configurations generated by the Co-designer may contain missing fields, illegal parameter values, or incompatible HW/SW settings. RACO applies a rule-based validator to ensure that each candidate specifies a complete CGRA architecture, provides legal compiler settings for every FMCW kernel, and can be executed by the CHIA evaluation workflow.

If a candidate fails validation, the \textbf{CGRA Fixer} receives the configuration and the corresponding error report, then revises the offending fields while preserving the remaining design choices. This repair loop continues until the candidate passes validation or the repair budget is exhausted. Only valid configurations proceed to selection, preventing malformed or infeasible candidates from consuming mapper evaluations.

\subsection{Multi-Judge Selection}

RACO ranks validated candidates through a two-level hierarchical selection process. The \textbf{Coarse Judge} screens the candidate set using the proposed HW/SW configurations and the evaluation history accumulated across previous rounds, retaining designs likely to improve the pipeline-level objective. The \textbf{Fine Judge} then performs a detailed comparison of the shortlisted candidates, assessing the interaction between the shared CGRA resources and the kernel-specific compiler transformations. It ranks the resulting designs and prioritizes candidates for evaluation within the available CHIA mapper budget.

The two judges constitute the search policy rather than the performance evaluator. Their predictions determine which candidates receive tool evaluation, whereas CHIA measurements establish mapping feasibility and final design quality. This separation focuses the limited evaluation budget on promising regions of the design space while preserving tool-derived results as the basis for design selection.


\subsection{Performance Evaluation \& Feedback}

For each candidate \(d\) selected for evaluation, CHIA invokes an LLVM-based CGRA toolchain~\cite{tan2021opencgra} to compile and map the five FMCW kernels using the proposed shared architecture and kernel-specific compiler settings \(u_{d,k}\). The toolchain extracts a dataflow graph for each kernel and reports its mapping status, initiation interval \(\mathrm{II}_{d,k}\), and diagnostic information. A mapping failure in any kernel renders the complete candidate infeasible.

RACO compares feasible candidates using a frame-level objective that accounts for the different workloads of the FMCW kernels. Let \(G_k(u_{d,k})\) denote the number of scheduled loop groups required by kernel \(k\) per frame under compiler setting \(u_{d,k}\). The estimated frame-cycle cost of candidate \(d\) is

$$
\widehat{C}_{\mathrm{frame}}(d)
=
\sum_{k \in \mathcal{K}}
G_k(u_{d,k})\,\mathrm{II}_{d,k},
$$

where \(\mathcal{K}\) denotes the five FMCW kernels. CHIA records per-kernel results and aggregate cost; the next Co-designer prompt receives candidate configurations, validity, failures, and aggregate costs, but not per-kernel initiation intervals. Because the objective is derived from mapper-reported initiation intervals, it represents an analytical estimate of steady-state CGRA cycles rather than measured end-to-end execution time or physical performance.



\section{Evaluation}
We evaluate whether closed-loop HW/SW co-design improves the mapping efficiency of the FMCW pipeline under a fixed evaluation budget. We compare the complete RACO workflow with two baselines that isolate the contributions of joint HW/SW exploration and iterative multi-agent feedback. We further analyze the search trajectory to determine how CHIA measurements influence subsequent design decisions.

\subsection{Experimental Setup}

We evaluate RACO on an FMCW workload comprising 256 samples per chirp, 128 chirps per frame, and four receiver channels. We represent the pipeline using five kernel views supplied to the mapper: windowing, FFT, transposition, power accumulation, and CA-CFAR. Each view isolates the computational loop evaluated by the CGRA mapper. The co-design space includes array dimensions, functional-unit allocation, scratchpad banking and capacity, and unrolling and vectorization factors for individual kernels.

All methods use the same LLM, legal parameter constraints, CHIA evaluator, frame-cost model, and cache policy. Each receives a budget of 10 unique kernel-mapper evaluations; evaluating one complete design requires five such evaluations, and failures count toward the budget. We report the best feasible cost from one recorded seed (37), not a multi-seed distribution.

\textbf{RACO} jointly explores architectural and kernel-specific compiler parameters through the complete four-stage loop. \textbf{HW-only} uses the same loop and CHIA feedback but fixes the compiler settings, isolating the benefit of software exploration. \textbf{Single-agent} uses one agent to generate all HW/SW candidates before tool evaluation, without subsequent correction, selection, or feedback rounds. Where tractable, exhaustive search provides a larger-budget reference for the fixed design space.

The Co-designer proposes two \(4\times4\) HW/SW designs, and the Fixer replaces \texttt{FMul} with \texttt{Mul} in their FU layouts. The Coarse Judge retains both candidates, and the Fine Judge predicts that design A will perform better. This result shows that candidate selection must consider frame-level cost rather than individual mapper II alone.

\subsection{Cross-Round Feedback}

We further examine the closed-loop behavior in a separate two-round run with 20 unique kernel-mapping attempts. After the first round identifies design A at 38.73 million estimated cycles per frame, its configuration, validity status, and frame cost are returned to the next co-design round. The Co-designer subsequently proposes configurations with additional SRAM banks and larger unroll factors. Three second-round kernel mappings fail, and RACO retains the first-round incumbent, leaving the best estimate unchanged. This trace demonstrates that measured outcomes are incorporated into subsequent proposals and that CHIA prevents unsuccessful candidates from replacing the measured incumbent. Because the recorded feedback contains total frame costs but no per-kernel IIs, it does not support attributing the second-round changes to a specific kernel.


\subsection{Artifact and Reproducibility}

We release RACO as an open-source artifact at \url{https://github.com/zsjiang99/RACO}. The repository includes the executable four-stage workflow, five FMCW kernel views, CHIA evaluation scripts, experiment configurations, agent decision traces, and per-kernel mapper logs for the reported runs. The accompanying interface presents candidate configurations, stage-level decisions, mapping outcomes, and frame-cost breakdowns, enabling users to inspect the complete path from design generation to tool-based evaluation. The reported frame costs are estimates derived from mapper initiation intervals and scheduled loop counts.

\section{Conclusion}

We presented RACO, a four-stage agentic framework for CGRA HW/SW co-design targeting FMCW radar processing. RACO integrates HW/SW candidate generation, validation and correction, multi-judge selection, and CHIA-based mapping evaluation into a closed-loop workflow. Under a common budget of 10 unique kernel-mapping evaluations, RACO identifies a design with an estimated frame cost of 38.73 million cycles, 8.1\% lower than HW-only exploration and 6.6\% lower than the single-agent baseline. We release the executable workflow, complete decision traces, and mapper logs to support reproduction and further investigation. Future work will extend RACO with end-to-end execution measurements and post-synthesis PPA evaluation across a broader range of radar workloads.
